When you smoke a cigarette, it can be gone within a matter of minutes. You light it, smoke it, stub it out and move on.

But whilst the cigarette has disappeared from your hands, it has not disappeared from the environment.

The cigarette, and crucially, the filter, remains. It may be sitting on the pavement, hidden among the bushes or washed into a drain. What may seem like the end of a cigarette's use is also the beginning of a much longer afterlife.

October marks ‘Stoptober’, the annual NHS campaign encouraging people to stop smoking. The focus of the campaign is, understandably, the health benefits that come from giving up smoking, with changes to health noticeable after as little as 20 minutes. The campaign also references financial benefits and other personal impacts of being able to break a longstanding habit.

But there is another side of the cigarette story that is less discussed, because many people do not consider what happens to their cigarette when they finish smoking it. The reality is that it isn’t truly finished and instead has simply entered the next stage of its life.

In 2000, it was estimated that 1 in 3 people smoked. Since then, smoking rates have steadily declined, with around 1 in 5 people now smoking. Yet despite this change, cigarette litter still remains the most prevalent form of litter globally and a significant environmental problem. Ellipsis data across 3000 surveys in 24 countries all show the same story - cigarettes make up 70-90% of litter on average.

The risks associated with cigarettes, then, extend far beyond just human health. Their impact on the environment is less widely understood, partly because people may not know exactly what a cigarette is made up of or what can happen to it after being discarded. Another factor is that people often see individual cigarettes as small and relatively insignificant. However, it's this mentality that leads to billions of cigarettes being discarded every day, which makes their cumulative impact much bigger.

So what actually happens to a cigarette once it has been discarded? And how does something that is used for just a few minutes go on to have such a long lasting impact on the environment?

**The part we leave behind**

Most of us probably don't think very much about cigarette butts. Even if we don’t smoke, we probably see them everyday without consciously registering it. In fact, Ellipsis has found that we walk past 500 cigarette butts during an average daily commute in the UK. They’re small, they're familiar and they're easy to overlook.

Perhaps that's part of the problem. Cigarettes are designed to be disposable, with most people thinking that once the tobacco has been smoked, the remaining butt can be easily discarded. In fact, surveys by Hubbub and Ellipsis have found that a third of smokers (34%) believe cigarettes decompose in less than a year.

The reality is very different, with ‘filters’ one of the cigarette's key components also being one of its most environmentally persistent, remaining in the environment long after the cigarette has been smoked.

Most cigarettes contain filters which are made of cellulose acetate, a form of plastic. Specifically, it is a synthetic polymer with the appearance of cotton due to its tightly packed fibres. Further surveys from Hubbub and Ellipsis showed that 36% of smokers believe the filter is made from harmless cotton wool, while only 28% know that it is actually made from plastic.

It's this lack of awareness which is part of the issue. The fact that a lot of smokers do not know exactly what goes into a cigarette means it is a lot harder for them to understand the environmental consequences, let alone care.

Not only are the filters made from plastic, they are formed in a way that makes them even more likely to cause environmental harm. This is because they contain >10,000 individual cellulose acetate fibres which can easily break apart, but remain individually very resistant, and persist in the environment for a long time.

So, what might appear to be a small piece of organic paper or cotton sitting on the pavement waiting to disintegrate, is actually resistant plastic. But unlike a plastic bottle, which is visibly plastic and known to everybody, a cigarette filter often blends into the background and becomes easy to miss or forget about.

**From pavement to drain**

Imagine the journey a cigarette butt goes on after being dropped, for example on the pavement outside of a pub.

At first, it sits on the pavement, but as people keep walking over it, it gets flattened and squished until eventually the small white rectangle looks more like an intentional design choice than litter.

Now imagine it is kicked from the pavement towards a gutter. The next time it rains, the butt will be washed down into the nearest drain. From there, it can take a number of routes depending on the drainage system, breaking down into multiple parts, including that all-important plastic filter, eventually ending up in streams, rivers and other watercourses. Importantly, unlike most household drainage systems, outdoor storm drains typically go through no form of filtration before the contents end up in UK watercourses. This means any litter that makes its way into a drain, including from surface runoff, will also make its way directly into streams and rivers.

The environmental charity, Thames 21, runs annual river clean-ups and data collection events with support from the Environment Agency. In 2025, volunteers cleaned and surveyed 65km of riverbeds in the UK and found that 71% of litter was plastic with further analysis showing that cigarette butts made up 38% of the total litter findings. Ellipsis riverbank surveys have shown that this is not an anomaly and that cigarette filters consistently make up between 40-90% of riverine/esturine litter.

At this point, cigarette butts have changed from a small, innocuous piece of litter on the pavement to a considerable, pervasive plastic pollutant in the water system.

Even more worryingly, the filter carries with it much more than just the plastic it is made up of.

**A filter full of leftovers**

Cigarette filters were first introduced in the 1920s and 1930s, although at that time they were not mass-marketed as many consumers found them to be expensive and changed the taste of cigarettes. However, after papers were published in the 1950s proving a link between smoking and lung cancer, the cellulose acetate filters became a feature of standard cigarettes.

The filter was advertised as a way to make smoking safer because they catch some of the substances released when smoking. This was later debunked by a number of organisations who found that the filters did not, in fact, do anything to decrease the risk of disease, and instead were simply a marketing tool - and subsequent environmental disaster.

What is true, is that the filters _do _catch some of the substances released from smoking. But rather than being a health benefit, it actually means that the filters themselves can become containers for these substances.

When a cigarette is finished, the filter will contain residue from tobacco smoke, which can include nicotine, cadmium, lead, arsenic and other toxic compounds. Once the filters get wet, these chemicals can begin to leach into the surrounding environments. WHO says tobacco products can release more than 7,000 chemicals into the environment, including around 70 known carcinogens.

This makes cigarette litter a dangerous combination of both plastic and chemical pollution.

Once the chemicals themselves have transferred into the environment, the plastic fibres that make up the filter still remain. Over time, the filters are exposed to sunlight, water and physical movement which can cause the fibres to begin breaking away. Eventually, what started out as a recognisable cigarette butt can become a collection of plastic fibres and particles dispersed through the surrounding environments.

Having entered the water and soil, these fibres can then be encountered by organisms and become a part of wider ecosystems themselves. This combination of chemical leaching and plastic fibres means that cigarette litter can have significant measurable harm on wildlife. In controlled acute-toxicity experiments, chemicals from a single smoked cigarette butt in a litre of water produced 50% mortality in the fish species tested.

So, whilst smoking a cigarette can take only minutes, the remains it leaves behind can persist in the environment for years to come, and cause serious environmental harm in that time.

**The most littered thing** 

It's undeniable that a cigarette can have a harmful impact on the environment. But what makes them even more dangerous is the sheer volume of butts that are discarded.

Data from Ellipsis Earth has shown that approximately 4.5 trillion cigarettes are discarded into the environment each year. This is equivalent to:

12.3 billion cigarette butts discarded daily

514 million every hour

~143,000 every second globally

In fact, WHO describes cigarette butts as the most abundant form of plastic waste globally.

They are everywhere, but they are also easy to miss.

Crisp packets announce themselves as they are blown about by the wind.

Drinks bottles are obvious when they roll across a pavement in front of your feet.

A cigarette butt is only a few centimetres long and is often camouflaged on the pavement.

In some ways it is understandable to think that the environmental impact of such a small item is not as much as bigger, more obvious plastic rubbish. However, with thousands of cigarette butts discarded per second around the world, the cumulative impacts become increasingly significant.

While cigarettes are the most common item to be littered, they are no longer the only form of nicotine waste. As the way people consume nicotine changes, the waste they leave behind changes as well. Daily or occasional vaping has increased in popularity over recent years and is now higher than the proportion of people smoking cigarettes. Like cigarettes, they can introduce damaging chemicals and plastics into the environment, but they also introduce a different type of waste because they contain electronic parts and lithium batteries.

Another form of nicotine consumption that has grown in popularity is snus, which is a smoke-free form of nicotine in the form of a pouch that sits against the gums. However, just like the environmentally damaging cigarette filters, most of these snus pouches contain synthetic fibres such as cellulose acetate.

Whilst these products may not have reached the same environmental scale as cigarettes, they pose a wider question about what happens to nicotine products when they have served their purpose. Cigarettes are the most familiar example, but they are part of a wider problem. Nicotine products are often designed to be used and then discarded, but if this is not done properly they leave behind materials that remain in the environment for a long time.

**Why do people drop them?**

There is an interesting psychological difference between finishing a cigarette, and disposing of it.

People can carry cigarettes with them every second of the day, waiting for the moment when they need a smoke. But as soon as they have finished smoking, the relationship to the object changes. The cigarette has served its purpose.

Rather than being the thing they need it has now become the thing they want to get rid of. If there isn’t a bin immediately available, the pavement can become the easiest option.

People don’t necessarily make environmentally harmful choices because they don't care about the environment. Sometimes they make them because it is the most convenient option, or because they don’t realise that their choice can have such an impact on the environment.

Another factor that can have a big impact is disposal design.

In a 2025 litter survey of Wokingham, Ellipsis Earth found that cigarette butts made up 64% of all local litter. However, of the 101 bins present, none of them had an ashtray. In fact, Ellipsis data showed 10-15% _more _cigarette litter close to bins rather than further away. One possible explanation is that smokers were attempting to dispose of their cigarettes responsibly but had no suitable place to extinguish or dispose of them. The next convenient decision became discarding the butts next to bins. Ellipsis data has also shown that improperly designed bins can become litter magnets, actually increasing littering in their surroundings, and this is certainly the case for discarded butts where no ashtray is available.

**The Stoptober connection**

This brings us back to Stoptober.

The campaign is primarily about the health benefits of stopping smoking, and understandably so. However, making the decision to quit smoking also has the benefit of removing one of the largest and most persistent streams of small plastic litter at its source.

So whilst the environmental benefit may not always be the main reason someone decides to stop smoking, it does offer additional benefits to what is already a significant personal choice. The current Stoptober campaign understandably focuses on the health benefits of quitting, but there is also an opportunity to highlight some of the environmental impacts of smoking alongside them. Health will always remain the main incentive for Stoptober, but it is unlikely to be the only thing that motivates every smoker. Even if it is not enough to make someone quit altogether, it could be enough for them to change their behaviour and think more consciously about how and where they dispose of cigarettes. Or, perhaps, it could be one more reason they consider quitting in the future.

Ultimately, the environmental problem doesn't begin when a cigarette is dropped on the pavement. It begins much earlier, with the production of a product designed to be used for a few minutes and then discarded.

Every cigarette that isn't smoked means one less filter that needs to be produced, used and eventually disposed of.

And if enough people make that decision, the impact goes beyond the individual cigarette and beyond the personal benefit. A reduction in demand has the potential to affect the number of filters entering the waste stream in the first place and could even potentially prompt a redesign by manufacturers for a more environmentally friendly option for those who do continue to smoke

**Small choices, Big impacts**

A cigarette may only be part of someone's day for a few minutes, but its environmental afterlife is often much longer.

It can begin on a pavement and end up in a river.

It can start as a recognisable filter and break into hundreds of tiny plastic fibres.

It can carry with it, nicotine, metals and other toxic chemicals.

And because they are so small, they can be relatively easy to ignore or forget about.

We tend to think of environmental damage through big events or noticeable objects such as food and drinks packaging, oil spills, landfills, sewage dumping.

But, environmental damage can also be created by millions of small decisions acting together.

One cigarette butt doesn't look like much. Neither does the next one. Or the one after that.

Somewhere along the way though, those individual objects can become much bigger.

So this October, if you're thinking of cutting back or stopping smoking, remember that you are not only helping your own health but also the health of the planet.

And if you continue to smoke, perhaps the next time you stub out a cigarette, it's worth thinking about where it goes next. The cigarette may end when the smoking stops. But its afterlife is only just beginning.
