You wake up before your alarm. The room is already warm, the air heavy and unmoving after a night that never really cooled down. The bedsheets cling to your skin, and outside, the street is quiet but the heat is already impossible to ignore.

First, you reach for a glass of water, trying to quench the thirst that has built up throughout the hot night. Then, still half asleep and on autopilot, you reach for your phone.

What you read next may shape how you understand the weather outside. One headline reads “34C heat will return this week – making UK hotter than Barbados and Ibiza!” while another reads “Amber heat-health alerts issued as UK could see 10 consecutive days of temperatures over 30C”.

The weather is the same. The story is not.

One of these headlines conjures images of beaches, sunshine and summer holidays whilst the other evokes a sense of risk, disruption and a need for caution. Both of these articles are reporting on the same weather but each one is telling you a different story.

These small differences are more important than they may seem at first. In an age of busy lives, social media feeds and endless scrolling, many people don’t get further than the headlines. Subtle decisions over the language, choice of statistics and the tone of a headline can alter how the reader understands an event before they’ve even opened the article itself.

During the recent UK heatwaves of 2026, Britons have not only been experiencing extreme weather; they have also been exposed to competing interpretations of how to feel about it. I set out to investigate this in more detail by analysing headlines from a number of online publications between July 6th and July 19th, looking beyond individual headlines to identify patterns across the coverage as a whole.

**The power of a headline**

Headlines do more than simply summarise a story. They determine how the reader first interacts with a topic, whether it evokes an emotion, conjures a visual or creates a new question before they've even clicked a link. In a growing online world, dominated by seconds-long social media clips and instant search results, headlines are often the only form of news that gets through the gaps.

For weather stories in particular, first impressions really matter. A heatwave can be framed as a welcome period of sunshine, a public health emergency, a consequence of climate change or just another inconvenience that must be worked through. The choice of framing in a headline can influence not just how people feel about the weather but also what they feel should be done about it.

Heatwaves in particular occupy a unique space in public perception. Despite being classified as a natural disaster, many members of the public associate some positive feelings with heatwaves compared to floods and storms, which are exclusively negative events. For some, hot weather means beaches, barbecues and long evenings outdoors. For others, it means sleepless nights, increased pressure on the NHS, infrastructure failures and environmental damage. These contrasting sides of a heatwave mean that journalists often have to choose which version of the story to tell.

These competing narratives are always present, but they were especially visible during the UK heatwaves of 2026. To explore these differences, I collected and categorised headlines from seven different publications and broadcasters between 6th-19th July. Rather than analysing the full articles, I focused on the first impressions given to readers through the headline alone. Which stories were prioritised? What tone/language was chosen? How often was climate change mentioned?

Focusing on headlines alone cannot tell us everything about a publication's coverage. An article may extensively discuss ideas that never appear in the headline, while a headline may emphasise themes which receive little attention in the main body. However, because headlines are the first, and sometimes only, way that readers engage with a story, they are still an important lens for understanding how extreme weather is presented.

**What makes a heatwave story?**

First, I wanted to answer the simplest question: what counts as a heatwave story?

Every publication covered the heatwave in some form, but the angles and aspects of the story they chose to focus on varied. Some headlines directly addressed the heatwave itself with mention of record-breaking temperatures, heat-health alerts and forecasts. Others focused on the consequences of the heatwave, from hosepipe bans and travel disruption to practical advice and lifestyle adaptations.

**How heatwaves are covered in the news: Direct vs Indirect** 

A headline such as “Amber heat health alerts in effect as UK set for one of longest-lasting heatwaves since 1976” clearly frames extreme heat as the main story with reference to the scale of the event and possible associated health risks. In contrast, the headline “The best fans to keep you cool in a heatwave” is reporting on the same event but does not give any sense of urgency or danger and instead presents the heatwave as a problem that can be solved through preparation and consumption.

Neither type of coverage is inherently ‘better’ than the other. Readers need practical advice on coping with extreme heat, but they also need to be aware about the serious nature of heatwaves. Instead, it is the balance between these types of coverage that is key.

When comparing individual publications, Sky and ITV had the highest proportion of articles directly focused on the heatwave (100% and 77%) with coverage including forecasts, alerts and public impacts. In contrast, The Telegraph and The Sun had a higher proportion of indirect articles (53% and 48%) which focused on lifestyle, consumer advice and product reviews.

The distinction between indirect and direct articles is important because they can create very different understandings of extreme heat. The question, therefore, is not simply whether publications covered the heatwave - they all did. The more revealing question is what role they gave the heatwave within their coverage: a crisis to respond to, a disruption to adapt around, or a summer event to make the most of.

**Seven ways of seeing the same heatwave**

Next, I focused on the headlines specifically. Headlines are often less than 15 words, but they play a powerful role in grabbing the reader's attention and forming their first impressions of a story. Therefore, the choice of language in a headline is important for deciding what version of a story is being told.

Although all 7 publications were reporting on the same period of extreme heat, they did not present it in the same way. Some headlines positioned the heatwave as a dangerous and disruptive event with a large number of associated risks, while other headlines treated it as something to prepare for, adapt around or even enjoy.

**The missing climate context**

The table shows which stories the different publications chose to prioritise, but it also presents a new question: which stories were absent? Despite scientific consensus that rising temperatures and increased frequency of extreme weather events are linked to climate change, explicit reference to climate change was uncommon across the headlines analysed. **Of the 256 articles between the period of 6th-19th July, only 5 made some reference to climate change**, each with varying levels of explicitness. These references were most concentrated in The Guardian headlines, with 4 articles referring to climate change whilst The Independent referred to it through 1 headline. _The BBC, ITV, Sky, The Telegraph and The Sun made no reference to climate change in their headlines._

However, this does not mean climate change was completely absent from coverage. Some headlines referred to wider environmental changes, including record marine heatwaves, increasingly extreme weather and the need to prepare for hotter summers. The distinction is that these headlines often described the consequences of a changing climate without explicitly naming climate change as the cause.

This reflects one of the main challenges of reporting on extreme weather. A heatwave is an immediate event with visible consequences that are urgent and must be reported on. However, the longer-term processes that cause extreme weather events are harder to summarise within a headline. As a result, many readers may engage with articles and understand what is happening in the moment without being given the wider context for why these events are happening and what must be done in the long run to prevent them.

**Why does this matter?**

Heatwave coverage has the power to shape how the public sees and feels about extreme heat. This can be as a temporary inconvenience, a public health emergency or as part of a wider pattern of climate change requiring long-term actions.

None of these interpretations are entirely wrong. The public do need practical advice and tips on how to manage the heat at a personal level. They also need to know what impact the heat is going to have on their daily lives through travel disruption, water restrictions and health alerts. However, when coverage focuses on these short-term adaptations, the wider climate context can be overlooked. The challenge for journalism is therefore not choosing between immediate advice and wider context, but ensuring audiences receive both.

The heatwave does not begin when we open our phones. It has already been there through the sleepless night, the warm room and the glass of water beside our beds. But the headline we read first influences what that heatwave becomes: a crisis, an inconvenience, a challenge to adapt to or a glimpse of a changing climate.

If extreme heat is presented only as a temporary inconvenience, the wider significance of increasingly frequent heatwaves risks being overlooked. If it is presented only as a crisis, audiences may become overwhelmed or disengaged.

The question is therefore not whether a heatwave should be described as a summer event or a climate emergency. It is both. The challenge is ensuring that readers are given enough context to understand not only how to cope with extreme heat today, but why these events are becoming part of the future we are preparing for.
